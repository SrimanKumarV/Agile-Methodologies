Requirement Engineering,
told through Karthik Supermarket

Karthik opened a supermarket. It grew fast — and started breaking in all the usual places: slow billing, vanishing stock, guesswork instead of numbers. Every formal idea in Requirement Engineering exists because of problems exactly like his. Follow his supermarket from chaos to a working system, one stage at a time.

🧾 Billing takes too long 📦 Products go out of stock 📓 Inventory in notebooks ❓ No clear plan yet
1 The Core Idea

Before a single line of code

Karthik walks into a software company and says one sentence. That sentence is not enough to start building anything — and figuring out why is the whole point of this chapter.

The Story

Karthik says:

"I need a Grocery Management System for my supermarket."

Can the developers start coding immediately? No. A single sentence hides dozens of unanswered questions — what should the system actually do, for whom, and how well? Someone has to dig those answers out, write them down, and confirm they're right before any development begins.

DEFINITION SLIPREQUIREMENT ENGINEERING (RE)
What it is
The systematic process of discovering, analyzing, documenting, validating, and managing requirements throughout software development.
In five verbs
Find them → Analyze them → Document them → Validate them → Manage changes in them.
Why it exists
To understand customer needs, remove ambiguity, avoid misunderstandings, cut wasted cost, and raise satisfaction.
STATUSREQUIRED BEFORE CODING

3 Stage One

Feasibility Study — "Should we even try?"

Before anyone touches the keyboard, the team asks whether Karthik's Grocery Management System is worth building at all — technically, financially, and in everyday practice.

DEFINITION SLIPFEASIBILITY STUDY
What it is
The process of determining whether a proposed software project is practical, acceptable to users, adaptable to change, and conformant to standards — before real investment begins.

Technical Feasibility

Can we build it?

Karthik wants a barcode scanner, online payment, and inventory tracking. The team checks:

  • Do we have the required hardware?
  • Do we have the right software tools?
  • Do we have skilled developers for this?
✅ Technology available → Technically Feasible

Economic Feasibility

Can we afford it?

The numbers are compared directly — cost to build versus benefit gained.

  • Cost to develop: ₹1,00,000
  • Expected benefit: ₹5,00,000
✅ Benefit > Cost → Economically Feasible

Operational Feasibility

Will people actually use it?

A perfect system that nobody uses has failed. So the team asks whether Karthik's cashiers are comfortable using computers at all.

  • Will staff accept the new workflow?
  • Does it truly solve the day-to-day problem?
❌ Staff resist computers → Can be Operationally Infeasible
4 Stage Two

Requirement Elicitation — "What do you actually want?"

"A Grocery Management System" is a starting sentence, not a specification. The developer now goes and talks to everyone the system will touch.

The Story — Stakeholders

People affected by the new system:

  • Karthik, the Owner — wants billing, sales reports, inventory control
  • The Cashier — wants a barcode scanner and product search
  • The Inventory Manager — wants low-stock alerts and supplier details
  • Customers and Suppliers — affected indirectly

Each of them hands over a different piece of the puzzle. Together, this is called Requirements Discovery.

DEFINITION SLIPREQUIREMENT ELICITATION
What it is
The process of gathering requirements from stakeholders — people affected by the software.
Also known as
Requirements gathering, using customers and existing system processes as input.

Six proven ways to pull requirements out of people's heads — tap each one open.

🗣️1. Interview
Developer meets a stakeholder directly. "How many products do you stock? How is billing done today? Do you need barcode scanning?"
+ Detailed, immediate clarification. − Time-consuming; different people may answer differently.
📋2. Questionnaire
A prepared list of questions goes out to many people at once. "Need online payment? Need a sales report? Need supplier management?"
+ Easy to reach many users. − Answers can be shallow or skipped.
👀3. Observation
Watch the current system in action rather than asking about it. Karthik's billing takes 5 minutes; stock is tracked by hand in a notebook.
+ Reveals the real, unspoken requirement. − Time-consuming.
💡4. Brainstorming
Owner, cashier, and inventory manager sit together: "Need reports." "Need faster billing." "Need low-stock alerts."
+ Generates many ideas fast. − Hard to manage a large group.
🖥️5. Prototyping
Build sample screens — a login page, a billing page — and let Karthik react. He asks for a barcode scanner and a discount feature once he sees it.
+ Very easy for a customer to understand and react to. − Time-consuming to build.
🏬6. Domain Analysis
Study similar, already-working systems — like D-Mart or Reliance Smart's software — to learn common, expected features before asking Karthik anything.
+ Borrows proven ideas. − Only works if similar systems exist to study.
5 Stage Three

Requirement Analysis — "Let's untangle this"

Once requirements are collected from many people, they rarely agree with each other. Analysis is the clean-up stage: find the errors, conflicts, gaps, and duplicates before writing anything formal.

DEFINITION SLIPREQUIREMENT ANALYSIS
What it is
The process of examining, organizing, refining, and resolving conflicts among collected requirements.
Conflict
Owner: "Only Admin should delete products."
Cashier: "I also want to delete products."

Two stakeholders want contradictory rules for the same feature — analysis has to resolve who's right.

Ambiguity
Owner: "The system should be fast."

Fast how? 1 second? 5 seconds? 10 seconds? Vague words like "fast" have to be turned into a measurable number.

Incomplete
Owner: "I need billing."

Cash only? UPI? Credit card? Discounts? The requirement is missing the details that actually decide what gets built.

6 Sorting the Requirements

Functional vs. Non-Functional

Once the confusion is cleared up, every surviving requirement gets sorted into one of two families.

Functional Requirements

Describe WHAT the system should do
  • Add a product
  • Search a product
  • Generate a bill
  • Generate sales reports
  • Give groups of users separate rights

Non-Functional Requirements

Describe HOW the system should behave
  • Response time under 2 seconds
  • Secure login
  • Support for 20 concurrent users
  • Security, storage, performance, cost
  • Flexibility & disaster recovery
7 Choosing What Matters

Prioritization & Negotiation

Karthik's wish list is bigger than his budget: billing, a delivery app, AI-based prediction, a mobile app. Not everything can be built first — so requirements get ranked, and the rest gets negotiated.

HIGH
Billing · Product Search
MEDIUM
Sales Reports
LOW
Dark Theme · Animated Buttons
Negotiation, in one line

The budget only supports the basics for now. The developer and Karthik sit down and agree on what actually gets built in this round — that agreement itself is called Requirement Negotiation.

8 Stage Four

Software Requirement Specification (SRS)

Everything discovered, cleaned, sorted, and agreed upon now gets written into one formal document — in technical language the development team can build from.

The Story

Karthik spoke in plain language: "I need billing, and I want it fast, and it should be secure." The analyst rewrites this into precise, technical, testable statements the whole dev team can act on.

Models used to describe it:

  • Data Flow Diagrams (DFD): show how data moves through the system
  • Data Dictionaries: repositories describing every data item used in the DFDs
  • Entity-Relationship Diagrams (ER): the data, its relationships, and attributes
DEFINITION SLIPSOFTWARE REQUIREMENT SPECIFICATION
What it is
A formal document, written by a software analyst, containing all software requirements in technical language.
Sample: Functional
Product Search · Billing · Inventory Management
Sample: Non-Functional
Response time < 2 seconds · Secure login
DOCUMENTREADY FOR REVIEW
9 Stage Five

Requirement Validation — "Is this actually right?"

A written SRS can still be wrong. Validation is the final quality check before development truly begins.

The Story

The SRS claims the system should support 10,000 users. Karthik reads it and says:

"I have only 5 employees."

The requirement is technically written down — but it's incorrect for this business. Validation catches exactly this kind of mismatch before it becomes wasted code.

DEFINITION SLIPREQUIREMENT VALIDATION
Checked against
Can it be practically implemented? Is it correct? Is it complete? Is it unambiguous? Is it consistent?
Techniques
Requirements reviews/inspections · Prototyping · Test-case generation · Automated consistency analysis
