01 — CORE IDEA

What is the concurrent development model?

It represents software activities — analysis, design, coding, testing — as parallel processes that each move through their own states, instead of one strict sequence of phases. This is the process backbone underneath Agile.

Kitchen analogy: a chef doesn't wait for vegetables to be fully chopped before starting the sauce. Chopping, sauce-making, and grilling happen together, and cooks constantly call out updates to each other. That's the concurrent model — activities running together, communicating constantly, instead of waiting in line.

This is the visual that makes the difference click instantly: Waterfall moves in one straight line; the concurrent model runs several lines at once, with feedback loops.

Fig. 1 — In Waterfall, phases queue up. In the concurrent model, activities run side by side, each in its own state, staying loosely synced through communication (dashed arrows).

03 — VOCABULARY

The seven states, defined

Every activity (design, coding, testing...) has its own copy of this same state machine running independently.

inactive

The activity is planned but no work has started yet.

under development

Work is actively happening — building the thing.

under review

Peers or leads are checking the work for errors or gaps.

under revision

Problems were found; the item is being corrected.

awaiting changes

Paused, waiting on a decision or input before work resumes.

baselined

Approved and locked in as a stable reference version.

done

Fully complete — though it can still reopen later.

06 — WORKED EXAMPLE

The To-Do List App: nine steps, every state

Follow the Design activity for a simple To-Do app through its entire life, one state at a time.

inactive

Nothing has started

"We haven't started designing the To-Do app yet. It's just an idea on the backlog."

under development

The designer starts drawing

"Now the designer is actually drawing the screens: add task, delete task, mark complete."

under review

The lead checks the work

"The team lead looks at the design: 'Does this cover everything the customer asked for?'"

under revision

A gap is found

"Lead says: 'You forgot a due-date field.' Now the designer has to go fix that specific issue."

awaiting changes

Paused for a decision

"The fix is scheduled but not yet applied — maybe the designer is waiting to confirm whether due dates should show days or exact dates."

under development

Back to active work

"Once confirmed, it goes back into active work to actually add the due-date field."

baselined

Version 1.0 locked in

"The design is now approved as version 1.0 — locked in as the reference everyone builds from."

done

Fully finished

"Design work for the To-Do app is fully finished."

↻ two weeks later
awaiting changes

Even "done" can reopen

"The customer says 'Actually, I want a reminder notification too' — even 'done' work can loop back."

09 — TRADE-OFFS

Advantages & disadvantages

Advantages

  • Works for any type of software project, big or small.
  • Easy to understand — just a flowchart of familiar states.
  • Immediate feedback from testing, since testing starts as soon as a piece is baselined.
  • Gives an accurate, real-time picture of the whole project's status.

Disadvantages

  • Needs strong, constant communication between team members.
  • Requires the team to track many activities' status at once.
