1.Why Agile Writes Requirements Differently

Before we touch the Grocery Management System, it helps to understand why Agile teams avoid writing huge requirement documents at the start of a project. In older approaches (like the Waterfall model), a business analyst would write a single massive specification before any code was written — and that document often went stale the moment real users saw the software.

Agile instead breaks requirements into small, testable slices called User Stories. Each one is written from the point of view of a real person using the system, not from the point of view of the machine.

❌ Traditional / Technical Requirement

"The system shall provide product searching functionality with indexed lookup on the product master table."

Written for a machine. No one asks who needs this or why.

✅ Agile User Story

"As a Cashier, I want to search products, so that I can quickly generate bills."

Written for a human. Anyone on the team — even a non-technical stakeholder — instantly understands the value.

A user story is not a smaller version of a requirement — it is a placeholder for a conversation. The card only holds a short sentence; the real detail emerges when the developer, tester, and product owner discuss it before the sprint.

2.The User Story Format — and the INVEST Rule

Every user story answers exactly three questions, and nothing more at this stage:

QuestionAnswered by
Who wants it?The <Role>
What do they want?The <Functionality>
Why do they want it?The <Benefit / Value>

As a <User Role>, I want <Functionality>, so that <Benefit>.

The INVEST Checklist

Once a story is written, professional teams test it against the INVEST principle before accepting it into the backlog. This is the single most useful checklist you will use as a student and later as an engineer.

LetterStands ForMeaning in Practice
IIndependentThe story can be built and delivered without waiting on another unfinished story.
NNegotiableIt's a starting point for discussion, not a locked contract — details can be refined with the team.
VValuableIt delivers value to a real user or the business, not just a technical task.
EEstimableThe team has enough information to roughly size the effort (story points).
SSmallIt fits comfortably inside one sprint — big stories should be split.
TTestableAcceptance criteria exist so QA can prove it works.

3.Dos and Don'ts of Writing User Stories

✅ DO

  • Write from the perspective of one specific role (Cashier, Admin, Customer...).
  • Keep the sentence short enough to fit on an index card.
  • Always state the benefit — it justifies why the story is worth doing.
  • Attach acceptance criteria before the story enters a sprint.
  • Split a story if it needs more than one sprint to finish.
  • Use real, active verbs: "search", "generate", "update", "alert".

🚫 DON'T

  • Don't write stories as system-level commands ("The system shall…").
  • Don't merge two unrelated needs into a single story (e.g. searching and printing barcodes).
  • Don't leave the "so that" clause out — a story without a reason is just a task.
  • Don't specify the UI design or database schema inside the story itself; that belongs to design discussions.
  • Don't treat the written card as final — it will evolve after conversation with stakeholders.
  • Don't make a story so big ("As an Admin, I want the whole billing module") that it can't be estimated.
A quick smell test: if you cannot picture the one person who benefits from a story, or if the sentence needs "and" to join two features, it's probably written wrong — go back and split it.

7.Acceptance Criteria — Dos and Don'ts

Acceptance criteria are the conditions a story must satisfy to be called "Done." They turn a vague sentence into something testable. Take US-03 (Add Product) as our example throughout this section.

✅ DO write criteria that are:

  • Specific — "Price cannot be negative", not "Price should be valid".
  • Testable — a QA engineer can pass/fail it with a single test case.
  • Independent of implementation — describe outcome, not code.

🚫 DON'T write criteria that are:

  • Vague — "System should work properly."
  • Describing UI colors/fonts instead of behavior.
  • So numerous that the story can no longer fit a sprint (split it instead).

Definition of Ready vs Definition of Done — two terms students often confuse: "Ready" means a story has enough detail to enter a sprint (clear acceptance criteria, estimated). "Done" means it has been coded, reviewed, tested, and is potentially shippable.

✅ DO

  • Keep the backlog ordered — most valuable / most urgent items on top.
  • Re-prioritize regularly as business needs change.
  • Only detail the top of the backlog fully; leave lower items rough.

🚫 DON'T

  • Don't treat the backlog as a fixed contract signed at project start.
  • Don't let the Product Owner work alone — grooming needs the whole team's input.
  • Don't add implementation tasks (e.g. "create database table") as if they were user stories.

10.Estimation and Sprint Planning

Teams size stories using Story Points — a relative measure of effort, complexity, and risk, not hours. Most teams use the Fibonacci-like scale: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21. The gaps grow wider because our confidence in estimating large work shrinks.

Story points measure relative effort. A story worth "8" isn't "8 hours" — it means "roughly as complex as our other 8-point stories," decided by the team using a technique like Planning Poker.

Example Sprint Plan for the Grocery Management System

Sprint 1
Login, Search Product, Add Product
→
Sprint 2
Billing, Invoice, Payment
→
Sprint 3
Inventory, Reports, Supplier Mgmt

Notice the sequencing logic: Sprint 1 builds the foundation stories that later sprints depend on (you can't calculate a bill in Sprint 2 without products that were added and searchable in Sprint 1).

Why is this an 8-pointer instead of a 3? Because it touches multiple calculations (subtotal, tax, discount) and must integrate with both the cart (US-10) and invoice generation (US-12) — more moving parts means more risk, so the team sized it higher.
