Scrum & Self-Organizing Teams,
through the College ERP Project

Meera and Dev, two final-year students, get pulled aside by their college one afternoon: "We need a brand-new ERP system — student login, attendance, fees, results, everything. Can your team build it?" What happens next is a live walkthrough of everything Scrum actually is.

🎓 One ERP system 📋 A mountain of requirements ⏱️ A fixed deadline 🔁 One framework: Scrum
1 The Starting Question

Why Scrum?

The Story

Meera and Dev are handed the ERP requirements list and told: "Show us something in a month." They have two options.

Option 1 (Traditional): disappear for a month, show everything only on the last day. If the faculty says "change everything," they redo it all.

Option 2 (Scrum): show progress every week, take feedback, keep improving.

Which is easier to recover from if something's wrong? Option 2 — every time.

NOTE-01Definition

Scrum

Definition
A lightweight Agile framework that helps teams develop software in small increments, deliver value quickly, and continuously improve based on customer feedback.
Why It's Needed
Traditional development struggles when requirements change often, customers want early progress, teams need better coordination, and projects run late. Scrum solves this with small, manageable Sprints.
Memory
Build Small → Get Feedback → Improve
2 Don't Confuse These

Agile vs. Scrum

Agile gives the values and principles — but it never specifies exactly how a team should work day to day. That gap is exactly what Scrum fills.

The Analogy

A teacher says, "Study well and score above 90." Good advice — but which subject first? How many hours a day? That's left unanswered. Agile is that advice. Scrum is the actual daily schedule that makes it real.

Agile tells WHAT. Scrum tells HOW.

AgileScrum
A philosophy (mindset)A framework (working method)
Defines values and principlesDefines roles, events, and artifacts
Tells WHAT to achieveTells HOW to achieve it

Important: Scrum and Agile are not the same thing. Scrum is simply one of several Agile frameworks — Kanban, XP, and Lean are others.

4 Who's on the Team

The Three Scrum Roles

A Scrum Team has exactly three roles — no more, no less — and every one of them shows up in Meera and Dev's ERP project.

ROLE 1Owns the What

Product Owner

Owns the backlog, prioritizes it, and makes the key product decisions — turning raw user requirements into actionable work for the team.

ERP Story: the college's IT coordinator plays this role, deciding that Login and Attendance matter more than Parent Portal for now.

ROLE 2Builds the What

Development Team

A cross-functional team of developers, testers, designers, and other technical members needed to actually build the product.

ERP Story: Meera (backend), Dev (frontend), plus a tester and a UI designer — together they can deliver a feature start to finish.

ROLE 3Protects the How

Scrum Master

Ensures the team has everything it needs to ship work on time, communicates progress, and clears roadblocks — without commanding the team's technical decisions.

ERP Story: when Dev gets stuck waiting for server access for three days, the Scrum Master steps in and gets it unblocked by end of day.

5 Who Decides "How"

Self-Organizing & Cross-Functional Teams

NOTE-02Definition

Self-Organizing Team

Definition
A team that decides for itself how best to turn Product Backlog items into a working increment, rather than being told exactly what to do by someone outside the team.
Cross-Functional
The team holds every skill needed to deliver — coding, testing, design — without depending on people outside the team.
Memory
Trust Team → Team Thinks → Best Solution
The Story

College IT wants to dictate: "Use Database A." Meera and Dev know Database B performs better for this workload. Because the team is trusted to self-organize, they choose Database B — and performance doubles.

Nobody assigned Dev to the frontend and Meera to the backend either — they split the work themselves, based on their own strengths.

Cross-functionalSelf-managingNo external micromanagementCollectively accountable
6 The Actual Process

How Scrum Works — Building the College ERP

Scrum divides a large project into small, manageable Sprints. In each Sprint, the team plans, builds, reviews, and improves — until the whole system is done. Watch it happen, one requirement at a time.

The Full Requirement List

Student Login · Attendance · Internal Marks · Fee Payment · Timetable · Hall Ticket · Result · Parent Login

Can all of this be built in one month? No. So Scrum says: build one small part at a time.

1Product BacklogComplete To-Do List

Definition: a prioritized list of all features, bug fixes, enhancements, and work required to complete the product — owned and prioritized by the Product Owner.

ERP Example: Login · Attendance · Fee Payment · Timetable · Hall Ticket · Results · Notifications · Parent Portal — the complete list.

Think of it exactly like a shopping list: rice, milk, sugar, oil, vegetables, soap — the complete list is the Product Backlog.

Memory: Product Backlog = Complete To-Do List
2Sprint PlanningDecide What to Build Now

Definition: a meeting where the Scrum Team selects the highest-priority Product Backlog items and creates the Sprint Backlog for the upcoming Sprint.

ERP Example: with only 2 weeks available, the team can't build everything — so they pick Login · Attendance · Student Profile as this Sprint's work.

It's just like planning study before exams: 10 units, only 3 days — Day 1 covers Unit 1, Day 2 covers Unit 2, and so on.

Memory: Sprint Planning = Decide What to Build Now
3SprintSmall Development Cycle

Definition: a fixed, time-boxed iteration (usually 2–4 weeks) during which the Scrum Team develops a usable product increment.

ERP Example: Sprint 1 builds Login, Registration, and Forgot Password. Sprint 2 builds Attendance and Timetable. Sprint 3 builds Fee Payment.

Like eating a large pizza — you don't eat it all at once, you go slice by slice. Software gets built Sprint by Sprint.

Memory: Sprint = Small Development Cycle
4Daily ScrumDaily Progress Meeting

Definition: a short (15-minute) meeting where team members discuss progress, plans, and obstacles — answering three questions:

1. What did I complete yesterday?   2. What will I do today?   3. Is anything blocking me?

ERP Example: every morning, Dev says "Login page done." Meera says "Starting the database next." Someone else says "I'm stuck" — and help arrives immediately.

Memory: Daily Scrum = Daily Progress Meeting
5IncrementWorking Software

Definition: the working part of the software completed during a Sprint.

ERP Example: after Sprint 1, students can already log in and register — even though Fee Payment isn't ready yet. That's still real, working software.

Like building a house: Week 1 finishes the foundation, Week 2 finishes the walls — each completed portion is an Increment.

Memory: Increment = Working Software
6Sprint ReviewShow the Product & Get Feedback

Definition: the meeting where the completed Increment is demonstrated to the Product Owner and stakeholders to collect feedback.

ERP Example: "Sir, Login is completed." "Excellent — now add Google Login too." That feedback flows straight back into the Product Backlog.

Exactly like submitting your project to your guide, who says "Good — but improve the UI."

Memory: Sprint Review = Show the Product & Get Feedback
7Sprint RetrospectiveLearn and Improve

Definition: a meeting where the Scrum Team reflects on the completed Sprint and identifies improvements for the next one.

ERP Example: "What went well?" — Communication. "What went wrong?" — Testing was slow. "What should improve?" — Start testing earlier next Sprint.

Just like the thought after a semester exam: "I should have started earlier" — that reflection is a Retrospective.

Memory: Retrospective = Learn and Improve
The Complete Scrum Flow — from raw requirements to the next Sprint
7 Two Kinds of Process

Defined Process vs. Empirical Process

Before understanding why Scrum works the way it does, it helps to see the one big decision underneath it: can a process be planned exactly in advance, or must it be learned as you go?

NOTE-03Definition

Defined Process

Definition
A process where all steps, inputs, and outputs are clearly known and fixed before work begins. Everything is predictable — the same process produces the same result.
Formula
Known Inputs → Fixed Process → Predictable Output
Examples
Manufacturing bricks (same clay, same machine, same mould, every time). A cake-mix packet (add 200 ml milk, bake at 180°C for 30 minutes — same result every time).
NOTE-04Definition

Empirical Process

Definition
A process where knowledge comes from experience, and decisions are made based on observation, inspection, and feedback.
Formula
Observe → Learn → Improve → Observe Again
Example
Cooking biryani — you don't wait until the end; you keep tasting and adjusting salt, water, and spice as you go.
Defined ProcessEmpirical Process
Everything planned before startingLearn while doing
Fixed stepsFlexible steps
Predictable resultsResults improve through feedback
Changes are rareChanges are expected
Example: Brick manufacturingExample: Software development
Why Scrum Chooses Empirical

Can every ERP requirement be known before development starts? No. Can the college change its mind? Yes. Can developers make mistakes along the way? Also yes. A Defined Process assumes everything is knowable in advance — software almost never works that way. So instead of "let's build everything first," Scrum says: build a small part, show it, get feedback, improve, repeat.

Defined: Plan → Execute → FinishEmpirical: Observe → Learn → Improve → Repeat
8 How Empirical Actually Works

The Three Pillars of Empiricism

Scrum's empirical process rests on exactly three pillars — remove any one of them, and the whole idea collapses.

Pillar 1 — Transparency

Definition: everyone involved has a clear, shared understanding of the work, progress, goals, and problems. Nothing is hidden.

ERP Example: if Dev finishes a module but doesn't tell anyone, Meera might start coding the same thing again — duplicated work. Daily updates prevent that.

In Scrum: the Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, and Task Board are all visible to everyone.

Memory: Transparency = Everyone Sees Everything

Pillar 2 — Inspection

Definition: regularly checking the product and the process to catch problems early, instead of waiting until the very end.

ERP Example: a student who studies Unit 1, tests themselves, finds mistakes, and then improves before Unit 2 usually performs better than one who never checks their own work.

In Scrum: after every Sprint, the working software is demonstrated and checked against expectations.

Memory: Inspection = Check Frequently

Pillar 3 — Adaptation

Definition: making necessary changes based on what inspection revealed, to improve the product or the way the team works.

ERP Example: a cricket coach who notices a batsman keeps getting out on the same shot says "change your technique" — the player adapts.

In Scrum: if students find the Login page confusing, the team redesigns it in the very next Sprint.

Memory: Adaptation = Change for the Better

If work is transparent, it can be inspected. Once inspected, the team can adapt — that's the whole chain.
Scrum EventPillar SupportedWhy
Daily ScrumTransparencyTeam shares daily progress
Sprint ReviewInspectionCustomer inspects the product
Sprint RetrospectiveAdaptationTeam improves its way of working
Memory: T → I → A (Tell everyone → Inspect everything → Adjust and improve)
"Scrum doesn't try to predict the future perfectly. It builds a small piece, shows it, listens, and improves — again and again — because that's the only honest way to build something as unpredictable as software."
