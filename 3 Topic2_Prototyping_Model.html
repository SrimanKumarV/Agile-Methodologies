The Prototyping Model

What do you do when the customer says, "I'm not really sure what I want... but I'll know it when I see it"? You build them something to look at first. That's Prototyping.

🧪 Build a Sample First 🔁 Refine Repeatedly 👀 See Before You Build

1What is the Prototyping Model?

Let's build this up slowly, starting from a very ordinary, everyday idea.

Imagine you walk into a tailor's shop and say, "Make me a shirt — something formal, but comfortable." The tailor doesn't cut the final expensive fabric right away. Instead, they first stitch a rough sample using cheap cloth, let you try it on, and ask, "Is this the fit and style you meant?"

You look at it and say, "Actually, make the sleeves a little longer." The tailor adjusts the sample. You try again. Only once you're happy with the sample does the tailor cut the actual, final shirt.

This is exactly what the Prototyping Model does in software. Instead of guessing what the customer wants and building the entire final software directly, the team first builds a quick, rough working sample — called a prototype — so the customer can react to something real.

Formal Definition The Prototyping Model is a Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) model in which a prototype (an early, rough, working sample of the final product) is built first, shown to the customer, tested, and refined again and again based on their feedback — until the customer is fully satisfied. Only then is the real, final system built.
📝
Remember

A prototype is not the final software. It usually shows only the important visible parts — like the screen layout, inputs, and outputs — not the full, detailed internal logic.

2Why do we need the Prototyping Model?

This model becomes necessary in a very specific, very common situation:

  • The customer does not clearly know their exact requirements.
  • The requirements are changing quickly, even as discussions happen.
  • The team needs a way to confirm understanding before investing heavily in the final build.

If the team just started coding the "final" software based on a vague description, they would likely build something wrong — wasting time, effort, and money. A prototype avoids this by giving the customer something concrete to react to, early and cheaply.

💡
Example

A bank wants a "simple, modern" loan-application form but can't describe exactly what fields or steps they want. The team builds a rough clickable prototype of the form in a day or two. The bank staff click through it and say, "Actually, we also need a co-applicant section" — a detail that may never have come up in a plain conversation.

📌
Interview Point

Prototyping does support risk handling — but only for risks that are identified before development starts. Risks that appear after development has already begun are not handled well by this model.

3Where is it used?

  • 📲 New mobile/web app UI design — where "look and feel" matters a lot
  • 🏦 Banking & government forms — where the exact fields/steps aren't fully clear at first
  • 🎮 Game development — testing a rough playable level before building the full game
  • 🛍️ E-commerce checkout flows — trying different layouts before committing to one
  • 🧾 Any project where the customer says, "Show me something, and I'll tell you what to change."

4How does it work? — The 5 Phases

The Prototyping Model moves in a repeating loop of five phases.

① Communication

The developer and customer meet and discuss the overall objectives of the software — a normal conversation to understand the big picture.

② Quick Design

A fast, rough design is created — but only for the parts visible to the user, like input and output formats. It deliberately skips deep internal detail, because the goal here is speed, not perfection.

③ Modeling Quick Design (Building the Prototype)

The quick design is actually turned into a working sample. This gives everyone — including the developer — a much clearer idea of the real requirements, because now there's something tangible to look at.

④ Construction of Prototype

The prototype is evaluated directly by the customer. They interact with it and point out what's right and what's missing.

⑤ Deployment, Delivery & Feedback

If the customer is not satisfied, the prototype is refined based on their feedback, and the cycle repeats from Communication again. This refining loop continues until the customer is fully satisfied — only then is the final real system developed based on this last, approved prototype.

🏆
Exam Point

Always mention the 5 phases in order: Communication → Quick Design → Modeling Quick Design → Construction of Prototype → Deployment/Delivery/Feedback — and don't forget the refining loop back to Communication.

5–6Advantages & Disadvantages

✅ Advantages

  • Errors are detected much earlier, before the final build
  • Greater customer satisfaction and comfort — they see progress early
  • Easily identifies missing functionality
  • Easily identifies confusing or difficult-to-use functions

⚠️ Disadvantages

  • Requires heavy client involvement, which isn't always convenient for the developer
  • Can be a slow process — more time spent going back and forth
  • Frequent changes can disturb the rhythm and momentum of the development team

7Real-World Analogy

🎯
The Tailor's Trial Shirt

Just like a tailor stitches a rough trial shirt from cheap cloth before cutting the expensive final fabric, software teams build a rough prototype before investing in the final, polished product. Both save money and effort by catching mistakes on the "trial" version, not the "real" one.

🏠
The Model House

Real estate builders often create a small model or a sample flat before constructing hundreds of actual apartments. Buyers walk through the sample flat, give feedback, and only then does construction begin on the real units — exactly like a software prototype.

8–9Simple Example & Programming-Style Example

💡 Simple Example — College Result Portal

The college wants a "Result Portal" but isn't sure how it should look. The team builds a quick prototype with just: a Roll Number box, a Submit button, and a dummy result table. The Principal tries it and says, "We also need to show the semester and CGPA." The team updates the prototype and shows it again. After two or three rounds, everyone agrees — and only then does the team build the real, fully working Result Portal with an actual database.

prototype-vs-final.txt 
// PROTOTYPE version — rough, fake data, just to check the LOOK and FLOW
function getResult(rollNumber) {
    // no real database yet — just returns fixed dummy data
    return { name: "Sample Student", cgpa: "8.5" };
}

// FINAL version — built only AFTER the customer approves the prototype
function getResult(rollNumber) {
    return database.query("SELECT * FROM results WHERE roll = ?", rollNumber);
}
⚠️
Common Mistake

Students often think the prototype code is reused as-is in the final system. In reality, the prototype is mainly a visual/functional sample — the final system is usually built properly from a solid design, often written fresh, using the prototype only as a reference for what the customer wants.

10Common Mistakes Students Make

  • ❌ Thinking the prototype is the final software — it's only a rough sample.
  • ❌ Forgetting that Quick Design covers only user-visible aspects, not full internal detail.
  • ❌ Believing this model handles all risks — it only handles risks identified before development starts.
  • ❌ Skipping the "loop back to Communication" step when drawing the diagram in exams.

📌 Chapter Summary

  • Prototyping Model builds a rough working sample first, before the final software
  • Used when requirements are unclear or changing quickly
  • 5 Phases: Communication → Quick Design → Modeling Quick Design → Construction of Prototype → Deployment/Delivery/Feedback
  • The cycle repeats until the customer is fully satisfied
  • Pros: early error detection, higher satisfaction  |  Cons: heavy client involvement, can be slow

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Is the prototype thrown away after use?

Often yes — many prototypes are "throwaway" prototypes used only to clarify requirements. Some projects do evolve the prototype further, but it's more common to rebuild the final system properly.

Does the Prototyping Model handle all project risks?

No — only risks identified before development starts. Risks that appear mid-way are not well handled by this model.

Is Prototyping suitable for very large, complex systems?

It's more commonly used for the parts of a system where requirements are unclear — like UI/UX — rather than for an entire massive system end-to-end.
