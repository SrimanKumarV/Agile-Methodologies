1FOUNDATIONS

User Stories

Definition A user story is the smallest unit of work in an agile framework — a short requirement written from the perspective of an end user, mostly in non-technical language, that clearly defines the functionality the end user expects.

Instead of one large requirement such as "build an online shopping application," the work is broken into small, independently understandable pieces — one story per capability (register, login, search products, add to cart, pay, view order history). This keeps each unit small enough to discuss, estimate, and deliver within a sprint.

Why teams use user stories

  • They give the team an easy way to manage the backlog.
  • Because they're written from the user's perspective, the Product Owner can review them with users and stakeholders to judge which stories are valuable.
  • Their small size makes it easy to add new stories or reorder the backlog at any time.
  • At the start of a sprint, the Product Owner and team pull stories out of the backlog and commit to delivering them in that sprint.
Example Instead of: "Create a MySQL table for storing attendance" (a technical statement) — a user story reframes it as: "As a student, I want to view my attendance percentage, so that I can monitor my attendance."
2FOUNDATIONS

The 3 C's of a User Story

A user story is more than the sentence written on it — it is completed through three components:

ComponentWhat it means
CardThe story is written on a card (physical or digital); it can be annotated with estimates, value, and notes.
ConversationThe full details of the story come out through discussion between the team and Product Owner — not from the card alone.
ConfirmationAcceptance tests are defined to confirm the story is complete — this leads directly into Conditions of Satisfaction.
3FOUNDATIONS

User Story Format

A user story is always written from the end user's perspective, using a fixed three-part template:

Worked example — Student Login Summary: Student Login page
User Story: As a Student, I want to have login functionality, so that I can successfully use the Student Module.
Scope: Build a login page; student validation.
Pre-condition: Student should have registered in school to use login.
Non-functional requirements: Should work in all browsers; login should complete within 3 seconds.

Every story should answer Who? What? Why?

Nominate-a-video example (index card) As a returning user with a large friends list, I want to nominate one friend's video for an achievement so that all of our mutual friends can vote to give him a star.

Who? Returning user with a large friends list  |  What? Nominate a friend's video  |  Why? So friends can vote to award a star.

User Stories and the Product Backlog

All stories live in the product backlog. At the start of a sprint they move through a short pipeline:

Product Backlog
→
Product Owner & team pull stories for the sprint
→
Team discusses stories for clarity
→
Stories broken into tasks
→
Tasks estimated
→
Tasks placed in "To Do"
5DEFINITION OF DONE

Conditions of Satisfaction

Definition Conditions of Satisfaction are an effective tool that help developers know what the software should look like when complete, and gauge how close they are to being done. They are defined for each user story — some teams call this acceptance criteria — and can fit on the back of the same 3×5 index card as the story.
  • They help developers avoid declaring victory too early, by giving a concrete definition of "Done."
  • A user story is "Done" only when its conditions are satisfied, and this is demonstrated in the sprint review.

Worked example — Student Login (Given / When / Then)

Acceptance criteria are the conditions a product must meet to be accepted by the user:

Scenario 1: Student can log in successfullyScenario 2: Student cannot log in
Given I am on the login page
When I provide a valid username and password and click Sign In
Then I will log in successfully		 Given I am on the login page
When I provide an invalid username and password and click Sign In
Then I get the error "Please provide valid credentials"

Given = starting condition  ·  When = the action taken  ·  Then = the expected result. A single story can have multiple acceptance scenarios.

Worked example — Nominate a video for an achievement

Conditions of Satisfaction
  • A user can nominate a video for an achievement.
  • A user's friend is notified when their video gets an achievement.
  • A user can see all of the videos their friends have nominated.
  • A video with an achievement is displayed with a star next to it.
Exam focus Frequently asked directly as: "Determine the Conditions of Satisfaction for <a given user story>." Answer by listing concrete, testable statements — each describing one observable fact that must be true for the story to count as done.
6QUALITY CHECK

Characteristics of a Good User Story

A good user story should fulfill this checklist:

  • Keep them short
  • Keep them simple
  • Write from the perspective of the end user
  • Make sure the value / reason for the story is clear
  • The story should have user acceptance criteria to test the functionality
  • It should be testable
  • The story should be linked to its related Epic (a collection of user stories)
Exam focus List any relevant points from the checklist above with a one-line justification each.
7ESTIMATION

Story Points

Definition Story points are units of measure for expressing an estimate of the overall effort required to fully implement a product backlog item or any other piece of work.

How points are assigned

  • Story points are relative — the team compares a current story to a similar one it has completed before, and assigns it the same number of points.
  • Teams use different numeric scales: 1–5, 1–10, the Fibonacci series (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13…), or exponential numbers.
  • Points are not hours — a story with 8 points isn't "8 hours of work"; it simply means the team judges it to require roughly proportionally more effort than a 5-point story.

Estimation process (sprint planning)

Start with the most valuable backlog stories
→
Pick the smallest story as a baseline
→
Find a similarly sized past story, assign the same points
→
Team discusses accuracy of the estimate
→
Repeat until enough points fill the sprint

Additional problems, work, or technical changes increase the estimate; reusing existing code decreases it.

Why story points work

  • They are simple, and not "magic" — the team stays in control of them.
  • They get the team talking about estimates, without developers feeling threatened by them.
  • They help the team discover exactly what a story means, and everyone becomes genuinely committed to it.
8ESTIMATION

Velocity

Definition Velocity is a measure of how much work is completed by the developers within a specific sprint. Teams tend to work at a fairly constant velocity, so past velocity is used to plan the next sprint.
Actual Velocity = Total Story Points Completed ÷ Number of Sprints
Worked example A team completes a total of 80 story points over 4 sprints.
Velocity = 80 ÷ 4 = 20 points/sprint
Worked example — multiple sprints Sprint 1 = 50 points, Sprint 2 = 25 points, Sprint 3 = 35 points.
Total = 50 + 25 + 35 = 110 points over 3 sprints.
Velocity = 110 ÷ 3 ≈ 36.67 points/sprint
9SPRINT TRACKING

Burndown Chart

Definition A burndown chart is a visual representation of the remaining amount of work for a specific sprint at the Scrum team level — showing, at a glance, how the sprint is progressing compared with the team's past velocity.

It is a near-essential Scrum tool because it helps with:

  • Monitoring project scope creep
  • Keeping the team running on schedule
  • Comparing planned work against the team's actual progression

Constructing the chart

  • X-axis: dates, starting on the first day of the sprint and ending on the last day.
  • Y-axis: story points, ranging from 0 up to 20% more than the total points in the sprint backlog.
  • Plot the first point at day 0 = total points in the sprint.
  • Draw a straight guideline from that first point to zero points at the end of the sprint.
  • As each story finishes and moves to "Done," plot the points remaining on the current day — the chart can equally be built with hours or days instead of story points.

Reading the chart

  • As soon as the first story is finished and moved to "Done," plot the points remaining at the current day; keep plotting as more stories complete and points burn off.
  • Scope creep: if the Product Owner adds stories mid-sprint, or the team discovers extra work in a Daily Scrum, remaining points can go up instead of down.
  • Gap between actual and guideline: a growing gap means there's a good chance the team won't finish all planned stories by the end of the sprint.
Exam focus — worked numerical A 2-week sprint starts with 50 story points. By the end of week 1, 15 points are completed. On day 4, 20 new points are added.
Remaining after week 1: 50 − 15 = 35 points.
After the addition: 35 + 20 = 55 points remaining — plot this as an upward jump on the burndown chart, then continue reading whether the actual line converges back toward the guideline by the sprint's end.
10SPRINT TRACKING

Interpreting the Burndown: No Risk, Low Risk, High Risk

Comparing the actual burndown line against the ideal guideline classifies the sprint's health:

SituationWhat the actual line showsInterpretation
No riskActual line closely follows the guidelineTeam is progressing as expected
Low riskSome deviation, but recoverableTeam may still complete the sprint
High riskSignificant, sustained deviation above the guidelineStrong chance not all stories will be completed within the sprint
11EXECUTION

Planning and Running a Sprint

The second half of sprint planning is about breaking committed stories into individual development tasks:

  • The Scrum Master starts with the first story and leads a discussion on exactly what needs to be built.
  • The team lists individual tasks, each expected to take no more than a day.
  • Each task is written on its own card — some teams color-code story cards separately from task cards — and grouped with its parent story.
Example — "Nominate a video for an achievement" Broken into tasks: modify the database schema to add an achievement table and stored procedures · create front-end web pages for editing and displaying achievements · add API service calls for adding, updating, removing, and finding achievements.
12EXECUTION

The Task Board

Stories and their tasks are grouped and placed in the "To Do" column of the task board, then move across the board as work progresses:

  • When a member finishes a task, they move it to Done, then pull the next task from To Do, write their name on it, and move it to In Progress (moving the parent story too, if it's still in To Do).
  • When the final task for a story is done, the member verifies all Conditions of Satisfaction are met before moving the story card to Done.
  • If a condition isn't met, the story moves back to In Progress and new tasks are added to To Do to finish the remaining work.

End of sprint

  • The sprint ends when its timebox expires — cards may still remain in To Do or In Progress.
  • Incomplete stories return to the product backlog to be re-prioritized in the next sprint planning session; the team may reduce their point value based on remaining tasks.
  • A story earns credit only once its card and all its task cards are in Done.
Generally Accepted Scrum Practices (GASPs) Practices adopted by many teams that aren't core Scrum rules — e.g., holding the Daily Scrum as a standup meeting where everyone remains standing until it ends.
